Scaling up multispectral color filters with binary lithography and reflow (BLR)

Nanophotonics · 2024
DOI: 10.1515/nanoph-2024-0090

Summary

Multispectral imaging requires arrays of filters with different optical thicknesses, but producing many thickness levels can involve repeated fabrication steps or specialized grayscale exposure. This work introduces binary lithography and reflow (BLR), which encodes the desired polymer thickness in the local filling fraction of a binary pattern. Heating redistributes the patterned polymer into continuous films, and adding metallic mirrors converts these films into Fabry–Pérot transmission filters. The experiments demonstrate at least 16 distinct filters using a single binary patterning process with a minimum feature size of 0.6 µm. Dielectric thicknesses span approximately 90–230 nm in increments of about 15 nm, producing different visible transmission bands. Optical microscopy, surface profiling and spectroscopy connect the patterned geometry to the final filter response. The approach provides a practical route to manufacturing arrays of spectral channels while simplifying control of the cavity thickness.

Sb2Te3–Bi2Te3 Direct Photo–Thermoelectric Mid-Infrared Detection

Advanced Optical Materials · 2024
DOI: 10.1002/adom.202401450

Summary

This study combines wavelength-selective absorption and thermoelectric conversion in a thin-film mid-infrared detector. A junction between Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3 serves as both the light-sensitive region and the thermocouple. An underlying reflector and dielectric stack create a Fabry–Pérot resonance that concentrates absorption near the junction, generating a temperature difference and an electrical voltage through the Seebeck effect. The surrounding device absorbs less strongly, helping localize the heating without a suspended membrane. Fabricated detectors centered near 3.6 µm reach a voltage responsivity of 10.2 V/W, a specific detectivity of approximately 4.6 × 10⁶ cm Hz½/W and a bandwidth on the order of 1 kHz. Optical and thermal modeling support the measured spectral and electrical behavior. By adjusting the resonant stack, the design offers a route to compact, spectrally selective thermoelectric detectors for infrared sensing.

Interband plasmonic nanoresonators for enhanced thermoelectric photodetection

Nanophotonics · 2025
DOI: 10.1515/nanoph-2024-0752

Summary

Efficient thermoelectric photodetection requires strong optical absorption where a useful temperature difference can develop. Here, interband plasmonic resonators are patterned directly into Sb2Te3 and Bi2Te3, allowing the same materials to provide optical confinement and thermoelectric conversion. Arrays of nanoscale posts enhance visible-light absorption around the thermocouple junction, while the surrounding electrode and substrate geometry control heat flow and electrical readout. The fabricated structures achieve approximately 90% absorption, more than twice that of the corresponding unpatterned films. Device measurements show a response time of about 160 µs and a specific detectivity of approximately 3.2 × 10⁶ Jones. Microscopy and optical simulations link this performance to the patterned resonators. The study demonstrates a solid-state route to integrating an optical absorber with a thermoelectric detector, reducing the need for a separate absorbing material and supporting further development of compact visible-light sensors.

Optical Wireless Power Transmission using Thermoelectric Device with Thin-Film Absorber

IEEE Journal on Wireless Power Technologies · 2026
DOI: 10.1109/JWPT.2026.3682232

Summary

Optical wireless power can deliver energy to a receiver without a wired electrical connection. This work explores a photothermal receiver in which an interference-based thin-film absorber captures laser light and supplies heat to a thermoelectric device. The thin-film coating controls optical absorption, while the resulting temperature difference is converted into electrical power by the thermoelectric elements. Experiments using a 450 nm laser with an incident power of 1 W produce approximately 2.0 mW of electrical output. The study also examines how heat spreads through the receiver: elements outside the directly illuminated region can contribute to power generation, so the optical spot and the active thermoelectric area need not coincide exactly. These results establish a proof of concept for thin-film-assisted thermoelectric optical power reception. Hybrid energy harvesting and small wireless systems are prospective applications rather than demonstrated deployed products.
